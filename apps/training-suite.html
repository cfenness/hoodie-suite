How Seeing Works · Lesson 01

The Visual System

Data visualization works because vision is the highest-bandwidth channel into the brain. Perceptual science is the study of what that channel reads accurately — and what it quietly gets wrong.

Roughly a third of the cortex is devoted to vision. The optic nerve carries on the order of 10⁷ bits per second — far more than hearing or touch. A chart is an attempt to load quantitative structure into that channel. The discipline exists because the channel is not neutral: it is exquisitely tuned for some judgments and systematically biased on others, and those biases are stable, measurable, and largely outside conscious control.

Two systems, two speeds

Vision operates in two modes. Bottom-up processing is fast, parallel, and automatic — it runs across the entire visual field in a fraction of a second before you've "looked" at anything (the next lesson). Top-down processing is slow, serial, and effortful — directed attention, reading, deliberate search. Good visual design moves work from the slow system to the fast one: it lets the eye see the answer rather than search for it.

The fovea is tiny

High-acuity vision exists only in the fovea — about 2° of visual angle, roughly a thumbnail at arm's length. Everything else is low-resolution periphery, good at motion and gross luminance, poor at detail and color. The eye compensates with saccades (about three jumps per second), stitching a high-res illusion from a narrow aperture. The practical consequence: a viewer never sees a whole chart at once — they sample it. Design that requires holding many precise values in view simultaneously fights the hardware.

Why this course is first

Representation science (the four-classifier method) is a set of rules. This course is the reasons — the perceptual facts that make those rules true rather than arbitrary. You can apply the rules without this; you can only defend and extend them with it.

Takeaway

Vision is a measurement instrument with known, stable biases. Effective visualization plays to the instrument's accurate readings and avoids its systematic errors.

How Seeing Works · Lesson 02

Preattentive Processing

Certain visual properties are detected before attention is deployed — in parallel, across the whole field, in roughly 200–250 ms. This is the fast system, and it is the single most exploitable fact in visualization.

Treisman & Gelade's Feature Integration Theory (1980) established the boundary. A target defined by a single primitive feature — a red dot among blue, a long line among short — "pops out": detection time is flat regardless of how many distractors surround it (parallel search). A target defined by a conjunction of features — the one red vertical among red horizontals and blue verticals — must be found by serial scan: time rises linearly with set size, ~40–50 ms per item.

Try it · serial search vs. pop-out

One T hides among the Ls (a shape conjunction — serial). Find it. Then let a single feature do the work.

Serial: search time scales with the number of items.

The preattentive feature set is small and well-catalogued (Healey & Enns, 2012): hue, luminance, orientation, size, curvature, motion, and a few others. Encode the one thing that matters in a preattentive feature and the viewer sees it instantly; encode it in anything else and they must hunt.

Takeaway

Pop-out is flat-time; conjunction search is linear-time. The signal belongs in a single preattentive feature — that is the perceptual basis for "grey the field, color the one thing."

How Seeing Works · Lesson 03

The Visual Variables

Jacques Bertin's Sémiologie Graphique (1967) gave us the alphabet: the retinal variables through which any mark carries meaning — and a framework for what each one can and cannot honestly express.

Bertin classified each variable by four properties. Selective: can you isolate all marks of one value preattentively? Associative: can you group them while ignoring the variable? Ordered: is a sequence perceived as ordered? Quantitative: can you read a ratio ("twice as much") directly? A variable should only encode data whose structure it can honestly carry.

PropertyThis variable
Note

Position is the only variable that is selective, associative, ordered, and quantitative — which is why it sits at the top of every effectiveness ranking. Hue is selective and associative but neither ordered nor quantitative — which is exactly why encoding a number in color is a category error.

Takeaway

Each retinal variable has a fixed expressive range. Match the variable's properties to the data's structure — quantity to position/length, category to hue/shape.

The Accuracy Hierarchy · Lesson 04

Elementary Perceptual Tasks

In 1984, William Cleveland & Robert McGill did something rare: they measured how accurately people decode each encoding, and produced an empirical ranking. It is the most important single result in the field.

They ran controlled experiments asking subjects to judge magnitudes encoded different ways, then measured error. The resulting ordering, most to least accurate:

  1. Position along a common scale
  2. Position on non-aligned scales
  3. Length
  4. Angle / slope
  5. Area
  6. Volume
  7. Color hue / saturation / density

The same five numbers, encoded three ways. Read each before revealing — notice how effortless the first is and how the last resists you:

Takeaway

Encoding accuracy is empirical, not aesthetic. Put the judgment that matters most in the highest-accuracy channel available — position and length first, color and area last.

The Accuracy Hierarchy · Lesson 05

Magnitude & Psychophysics

Why is area near the bottom? Because of a law older than data viz. S. S. Stevens' power law (1957) describes how perceived magnitude relates to physical magnitude — and for area, the relationship is badly compressive.

Stevens found that perceived intensity ψ scales as a power of the stimulus φ: ψ = k · φⁿ. The exponent n is the whole story. For length, n ≈ 1.0 — perception tracks reality almost exactly. For area, n ≈ 0.7 — perception compresses: a circle with three times the area looks only about twice as big. This is not a mistake people can train away; it is how the system encodes magnitude.

Try it · estimate the ratio, then meet the power law
B is this many times the area of A: 2.0×
Common trap · the radius error

Doubling a bubble's radius to show "twice as much" actually quadruples its area (πr²). The data says 2×; the ink says 4×; perception reads it as ≈ 2.8× (4^0.7). Two compounding errors in one chart. Always scale bubbles by area, never radius — and even then, expect underestimation.

Takeaway

Perceived magnitude is a power function of the real one, with a channel-specific exponent. Length is honest (n≈1); area lies low (n≈0.7); volume lies lower. The hierarchy is psychophysics, not opinion.

The Accuracy Hierarchy · Lesson 06

Effectiveness & Automated Design

Jock Mackinlay's APT (1986) turned Bertin and Cleveland–McGill into an algorithm. It is the direct ancestor of every "recommend a chart" feature in modern tools.

Mackinlay formalized two criteria. Expressiveness: an encoding must express all the facts in the data and only those facts (a bar chart of nominal data on a quantitative axis expresses a false ordering — it fails). Effectiveness: among expressive options, prefer the one the perceptual system decodes most accurately — and crucially, the effectiveness ranking depends on the data type. Position is best for everything; but for nominal data, hue beats length, while for quantitative data, length beats hue.

Try it · the effectiveness ranking shifts by data type
    Note · this is "Show Me"

    Tableau's "Show Me" is Mackinlay's ranking productized (he was a Tableau VP). But note what it does: it recommends an effective encoding, then lets you override into anything. Effectiveness as suggestion, not constraint. Hoodie's move is to run the same ranking as an enforced gate — the difference between a tool that helps and a tool that refuses.

    Takeaway

    Encoding choice is formalizable: expressive first, then most-effective for the data type. The ranking is not fixed — it rotates depending on whether the data is quantitative, ordinal, or nominal.

    Where Perception Betrays · Lesson 07

    Simultaneous Contrast

    Lightness and color are not read in absolute terms — they are read relative to surroundings. The same gray looks lighter on a dark field and darker on a light one. This is not an illusion to admire; it is a reason to distrust color as a quantitative channel.

    The retina encodes contrast, not absolute luminance — lateral inhibition between photoreceptors amplifies edges and makes every judgment contextual (Chevreul described it in 1839; it underlies the entire study of color appearance). The two center patches below are the identical gray:

    Try it · two identical grays that refuse to look identical
    Implication

    Colin Ware's work (Information Visualization, 2004) is blunt about the consequence: never ask a viewer to read an absolute quantity from a color whose surround varies. A choropleth where the same value sits on different neighbors will be misread. If quantity must ride on color, use a calibrated luminance ramp and a legend — and even then, treat it as approximate.

    Takeaway

    Color is a relative channel, not an absolute one. Context shifts the reading. This is the deepest reason color sits at the bottom of the accuracy hierarchy.

    Where Perception Betrays · Lesson 08

    Distortion & the Lie Factor

    Edward Tufte gave the most cited distortion a number. The lie factor (1983) = (size of effect shown in the graphic) ÷ (size of effect in the data). An honest chart has a lie factor of 1.0. Truncating a bar's baseline inflates it without touching a single number.

    Two values: 100 and 110 — a real difference of 10%. Drag the baseline up from zero and watch the apparent difference balloon while the data stays fixed:

    Try it · manufacture a lie without changing the data
    baseline: 0
    Common trap

    "But the truncated axis shows the difference more clearly!" It shows it larger, which is not the same as clearer — it shows a 10% difference as if it were 200%. For bars, whose entire encoding is length from a zero baseline, truncation is never honest. (Line charts, which encode change via slope rather than length-from-zero, can sometimes start above zero — a real distinction, not a loophole.)

    Takeaway

    Distortion is quantifiable. Lie factor should be ≈ 1.0. A bar chart's baseline belongs at zero because the bar's length is the quantity.

    Where Perception Betrays · Lesson 09

    Gestalt Grouping

    The Gestalt psychologists (Wertheimer, Koffka, Köhler, 1920s) catalogued how the visual system imposes structure — grouping marks into wholes automatically, by a handful of rules. These rules are a tool when used deliberately and a lie when triggered by accident.

    The eye groups by proximity (near = related), similarity (alike = related), enclosure (a shared boundary = a set), connection (a line between = related), continuity, and closure. Watch the same twelve dots regroup as each cue is applied:

    Try it · one set of dots, four groupings
    Common trap

    Accidental grouping. Inconsistent spacing in a legend implies categories that aren't related; a stray enclosure box suggests a set the data doesn't contain; similar colors across unrelated series imply a link. The eye will group — the only question is whether you control the cue or it controls the reader.

    Takeaway

    Grouping is automatic and rule-governed. Use proximity, similarity, and enclosure on purpose to show real structure — and audit your layout for the ones you triggered by accident.

    Designing for the Eye · Lesson 10

    Color Vision & Deficiency

    Roughly 8% of men (and ~0.5% of women) have a color-vision deficiency — most commonly red-green (deuteranopia / protanopia). A red-vs-green encoding isn't a minor accessibility miss; it is invisible to one in twelve male viewers.

    The chart below uses the classic worst choice — red vs. green categories. Simulate deuteranopia and watch the two series collapse into the same muddy tone; then switch to a deficiency-safe palette and watch the distinction survive:

    Try it · see your chart through a deuteranope's eyes
    Showing: red/green palette, normal vision.
    The fix is redundant encoding

    The robust principle (Brewer's ColorBrewer; Ware): never let color be the only carrier of a distinction. Pair it with position, shape, direct labels, or a deficiency-safe palette (blue/orange survives every common CVD). Redundant encoding also helps everyone in poor light, on bad screens, and in grayscale print.

    Takeaway

    Color-blindness is a design constraint, not an edge case. Encode redundantly and choose CVD-safe palettes — a distinction carried by hue alone is invisible to ~8% of men.

    Designing for the Eye · Lesson 11

    From Perception to Representation

    Everything in this course converges on one claim: the representation-science method isn't a set of preferences — it is perceptual science, operationalized. Each classifier is a perceptual fact wearing a rule's clothing.

    The bridge, classifier by classifier:

    • Measurement type is Bertin and Mackinlay — a variable may only encode data whose structure it can express (Lessons 02, 05).
    • Relationship → family is Cleveland–McGill effectiveness — the family that puts the key judgment in the highest-accuracy channel (Lessons 03, 04).
    • Cardinality & handling is preattentive processing — grey the field, let one preattentive feature carry the signal (Lesson 02).
    • Rhetorical verb is pop-out — an alert encodes the divergence in a single preattentive feature so it is seen before it is sought (Lesson 02).

    And the faithfulness gradient is the whole second half of this course: the lie factor (Lesson 07), the radius error (Lesson 04), simultaneous contrast (Lesson 06), accidental grouping (Lesson 08), and color-only encoding (Lesson 09) are precisely the perceptual failures the gradient exists to refuse.

    Takeaway

    You now hold the why. The representation-science course is the what and how, and every rule in it traces back to a result in this one. Perception is the foundation; representation is the method built on it.

    Foundations · Lesson 01

    What is Representation Science?

    A chart is not a picture of your data. It is an encoding of the structure underneath it — and like any encoding, it can be faithful or it can lie.

    When you make a chart, you map data onto visual channels: a value becomes a length, a category becomes a color, a date becomes a position on an axis. The reader then decodes those channels back into quantities. Representation science is the discipline of choosing an encoding the reader can decode accurately and without being misled — the one whose visual form matches what's actually true.

    Encoding, not decoration

    Every visual choice means something. Position on an axis claims proportional magnitude. A color gradient claims order. Area claims a true zero. If those claims aren't true of the data, the chart lies — even when every number in it is correct.

    Example

    Two bars, both accurate, placed side by side: the eye reads "comparison." If they measure different things on different scales — depletions next to retail scans — the numbers are right but the arrangement asserts a comparison that isn't valid. A lie told by the representation, not the data.

    Floor and ceiling, at once

    The discipline does two jobs. It raises the floor — stops you from shipping an analytically wrong or visually illiterate chart on your worst day. And it raises the ceiling — hands you a more expressive, honest representation than you'd have reached for alone.

    Note · why a gallery isn't enough

    A chart gallery lets you pick what you recognize — which caps you at your own taste. Representation science derives the right form from the data's structure, so the ceiling is set by the science, not by your familiarity with chart types.

    Takeaway

    A representation is honest when its encoding matches the structure of the data. The whole course is one instinct, applied at every layer: don't let the representation lie about the structure.

    Foundations · Lesson 02

    Encoding & the Visual Variables

    In 1967, Jacques Bertin catalogued the visual variables — the basic channels through which marks carry meaning. Every chart is a choice of which variable encodes which part of the data.

    The channels: position, length/size, shape, value (light–dark), color hue, orientation, and texture. They are not interchangeable. Each is suited to a particular kind of data, and using the wrong one is the first place a chart goes wrong.

    VariableBest forReads as

    Length / sizequantities (ratio)very accurate
    Value (light–dark)ordered dataordered, low precision
    Color huecategories (nominal)distinct, not ordered
    Shapecategoriesdistinct, not ordered

    Common trap

    Using hue to encode a quantity. Hue is categorical — red isn't "more than" blue. Map a magnitude onto a rainbow and you've asked the reader to decode an order that the channel doesn't carry. (More on this in the Color lesson.)

    Takeaway

    A chart is an assignment of data to visual variables. Match the variable to the kind of data — quantities to position and length, categories to hue and shape.

    Foundations · Lesson 03

    The Perceptual Hierarchy

    Not all visual variables are read equally well. Cleveland & McGill (1984) ranked the elementary perceptual tasks by how accurately people decode them. This ranking is why some charts feel obvious and others fool you.

    From most to least accurate: position on a common scale → length → angle/slope → area → volume → color/density. The implication is blunt: encode what matters most in the most accurate channel.

    Try it · let your eye get fooled

    Four markets, sized by area. Click the one you think sold the most.

    Note

    Area encoding is near the bottom of the hierarchy — the eye underestimates large areas and the differences compress. The same four numbers as bars (length on a common scale) are unmistakable. That gap is the perceptual hierarchy.

    Takeaway

    Put the most important comparison in the most accurate channel. Position and length for the thing that matters; reserve area and color for when nothing more accurate fits.

    Foundations · Lesson 04

    The Four-Classifier Pipeline

    "What chart should this be?" feels creative. It's mostly deterministic. Four classifiers, run in order, narrow infinite options to one or two honest cells.

    Each removes options that would lie. By the end, the only creativity left is craft inside the cell the classifiers hand you.

    1. Measurement type — what kind of quantity? Prunes the space.
    2. Relationship type — what is being shown? Selects the family.
    3. Cardinality & dispersion — how many, how spread? Forces the handling.
    4. Rhetorical verb — prove, compare, alert, orient? Tunes the emphasis.
    Note · order matters

    The order isn't arbitrary. Measurement type constrains everything downstream, so it's first; the verb only tunes emphasis, so it's last. Run them out of order and you'll defend a chart family the data never allowed.

    The next four lessons take one classifier each, with a live demo. The visual-variable and perceptual lessons you just finished are what the classifiers draw on when they choose an encoding.

    Takeaway

    Constrain first, generate second: measurement → relationship → cardinality → verb. Narrow to the honest cell, then make it beautiful.

    The Classifiers · Lesson 05

    Measurement Type

    Before anything else: what kind of quantity is this? Stevens (1946) named four levels of measurement, and each permits different operations — and different encodings.

    • Nominal — names, no order. Only equality. Brands, markets.
    • Ordinal — ordered, gaps unequal. Order, but no arithmetic. Well / call / premium.
    • Interval — equal gaps, no true zero. Add and subtract, but no ratios. Temperature, dates.
    • Ratio — equal gaps and a true zero. All arithmetic. Cases, revenue, volume.

    The level decides which encodings are honest. Only ratio may be encoded by area or count, because only ratio has the true zero those channels imply.

    Try it · pick a variable, see what's legal
    EncodingLegal?
    Common trap

    Treating ordinal as interval. "The average price tier is 2.4" is meaningless — there's no real, equal distance between well, call, and premium to average. And a sequential color ramp across tiers implies gaps that don't exist.

    Takeaway

    Measurement type prunes the option space. Area and count require a true zero — ratio only. The chart's first choice is the one most often wrong.

    The Classifiers · Lesson 07

    Cardinality & Dispersion

    Cardinality doesn't change the chart family — it forces the handling within it. The same "trend" is a clean line at n=3 and an illegible mess at n=60.

    And dispersion is the other half: how much the series disagree. Low dispersion can be summarized; high dispersion — series that diverge in sign — is the story and must not be averaged away.

    Try it · the 60-line problem
    3 series
    Common trap

    The "spaghetti chart" — twenty-plus lines, a legend nobody can map back. It's a valid line chart that communicates nothing. And its evil twin: averaging away high dispersion into one calm line that hides the divergence underneath.

    Takeaway

    Roughly: n ≤ 7 direct lines · 8–15 grey the field, highlight the signal · > 15 small multiples. Cardinality forces the handling; dispersion decides whether you may summarize at all.

    The Classifiers · Lesson 08

    The Rhetorical Verb

    The same honest data, shaped to the job the chart must do. This is the one input you can't read off the data — it comes from the claim.

    Four verbs: prove · compare · alert · orient. Switch the verb and watch the emphasis move — without any number changing.

    Try it · same data, four jobs

    The title carries the message

    A corollary, from Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic's storytelling grammar: the title should state the so-what, not the topic. "On-premise pour down 11%" beats "Pour by Channel." The chart shows; the title says.

    Note

    The alert view leads with the −11% against a baseline; orient lays all three out calmly. Truth is identical; rhetoric is set by the verb. Leading an alert with the reassuring +0.3% is technically honest and a lie of emphasis.

    Takeaway

    The verb tunes emphasis and ordering, never the numbers. Alert opens on the divergence; orient stays calm; the title says the so-what.

    Faithfulness & Craft · Lesson 09

    The Faithfulness Gradient

    Beyond which representation, there's how much visual ambition it earns. The rule: a representation earns elaboration in proportion to how faithfully the elaboration maps to the data.

    This single rule raises floor and ceiling at once. Low-faithfulness elaboration is denied (floor protected); high-faithfulness elaboration is unlocked (ceiling raised). Climb only as faithfulness rises:

    • Decoration — a bottle icon beside a number. Garnish. Fine in a divider, denied in a data slot.
    • Faithful iconography — the icon is the encoding. Ten marks mean ten times more (isotype).
    • Faithful infographic — composed encoding. Cluster glyphs whose shape encodes dispersion.
    • Structural diagram — swimlane, flow. Pure structure; almost impossible to lie with. Elaborate freely.
    Common trap · chartjunk

    Tufte's term for ink that carries no data. A clip-art bottle sized at random looks "designed" but encodes nothing. If size, position, or fill doesn't map to a value, it's garnish — and garnish belongs only in non-data slots.

    Takeaway

    Data-faithful or it's decoration. Hesitance isn't the tax for raising the floor — it's the mechanism that lets you safely raise the ceiling.

    Faithfulness & Craft · Lesson 10

    Risk-Weighted Hesitance

    Caution shouldn't be uniform. Calibrate it to how easy each representation type is to lie with. Highest where deception is easy; lowest where structure protects honesty.

    • Trusted — elaborate freely. Swimlanes, flows. They encode sequence and handoff; no "hypergeneralized swimlane" failure mode.
    • Standard checks. Bars, lines, tables. Well-understood, occasionally misused (truncated axes, dual axes).
    • Verify the mapping. Isotype, glyphs. Powerful when the unit maps cleanly; misleading when it doesn't.
    • Prove it first. Area, bubble, 3-D. The eye judges area badly and volume worse (recall the perceptual hierarchy).
    Note

    This is why a good system is least hesitant about swimlanes and most hesitant about bubble clouds. Caution tracks deception risk, not a blanket timidity — uniform caution would deny the expressive-and-honest forms along with the dangerous ones.

    Takeaway

    Calibrate hesitance to lie-risk. Structure protects honesty — trust it. Area fools the eye — make it earn its place.

    Faithfulness & Craft · Lesson 11

    Color — Use & Misuse

    Color is the most abused channel because it's the most tempting. There are exactly three honest palette types, and each matches one kind of data.

    • Categorical — distinct hues for nominal categories. No order implied.
    • Sequential — one hue, light→dark, for ordered/quantitative data.
    • Diverging — two hues meeting at a neutral midpoint, for data with a meaningful center (gain vs. loss, above vs. below).
    Try it · pick your data, see the legal palette
    PaletteFor this data?
    Common trap · the rainbow

    The rainbow (jet) ramp on sequential data. It has no perceptual order — yellow doesn't read as "between" green and red — it creates false boundaries where the hue shifts, and it's unreadable to the color-blind. Use a single-hue sequential ramp instead.

    Takeaway

    Three palettes, three data types: categorical for nominal, sequential for ordered, diverging for centered. Never a rainbow for magnitude.

    Faithfulness & Craft · Lesson 12

    Preattentive Attributes

    Some visual properties are processed before conscious attention — in under 250 milliseconds, in parallel, across the whole field. Use them and the signal "pops." Ignore them and the reader has to search.

    Preattentive attributes include color, size, orientation, and a few others. The classic demonstration: finding one different item is instant when it differs by a preattentive attribute, and slow when it doesn't.

    Try it · find the 5

    There's one 5 hidden among the 2s. Find it — then let color do the work.

    Serial search — you have to scan each cell.

    Gestalt grouping

    Related: the Gestalt principles describe how the eye groups marks — by proximity (near = related), similarity (alike = related), enclosure, and continuity. Good layout uses them on purpose; bad layout triggers them by accident, implying groupings that aren't real.

    Takeaway

    Encode the signal in a preattentive attribute so it's seen before it's searched for. Grey the field, color the one thing that matters.

    Domain & Practice · Lesson 13

    The Bev-Alc Traps

    Generic representation science gets you most of the way. The last mile is domain-specific — the traps that only matter once you know what three-tier depletion data actually is.

    • Depletions are not consumer takeaway. They're distributor-to-retailer movement. Never trended as one series against retail scan — different things, different scales.
    • A decline inside a coverage gap is an artifact until proven demand. Missing reporters look like a drop that isn't real.
    • A depletion spike may be loading or destocking, not velocity. Inventory movement masquerades as growth.
    • On-premise and off-premise aren't comparable lines. Different denominators, different behavior.
    • A flat depletion line is the most dangerous chart in the building. The calm top-line is guilty until the clusters underneath agree.
    Note

    Each of these is a representation rule, not just an analysis note. "Don't put depletions and scan on one axis" is a composition constraint. "Treat the flat line as suspect" is a cardinality/dispersion rule. The domain is where the generic science gets teeth.

    Takeaway

    The unliftable layer: the domain decides which honest-looking charts are actually lies. This is the part no horizontal tool can encode for you.

    Domain & Practice · Lesson 14

    Worked Example

    Run all four classifiers on one real question, end to end, and watch the option space collapse to a single honest cell.

    The question: "Our top silver rum looks flat (+0.3% depletions) but distribution is up. Is everything fine?" The data: on-premise well placements down 11%, feature/display holding.

    Step 1 · Measurement type

    YoY % change across segments — a ratio quantity. Length and position encodings are legal. Segment labels (well, call, feature, display) are nominal — categorical color, no ramp.

    Step 2 · Relationship type

    A flat headline that decomposes across segments that disagree, one deviating sharply down. Relationship = deviation / distribution, not trend → diverging-from-baseline family, not a line.

    Step 3 · Cardinality & dispersion

    Four segments — low cardinality, no spaghetti. But high dispersion: they disagree in sign. That's the signal. Handling = show all four against the zero baseline so the −11% is unmissable.

    Step 4 · Rhetorical verb

    Stop someone walking into a QBR reassured. Verb = alert. Lead with the divergence, render it diverging, and—from Lesson 08—put the so-what in the title.

    The honest cell

    Ratio + deviation + low-cardinality/high-dispersion + alert → a diverging bar leading with the −11%, baseline at zero, the divergence in a preattentive color, titled "You're not losing the shelf — you're losing the pour." Every other representation hides the divergence or misstates the relationship.

    Takeaway

    Four questions turned "make a chart" into one defensible answer — and the perceptual, color, and preattentive lessons decided how to render it. Narrow honestly, then render well.
